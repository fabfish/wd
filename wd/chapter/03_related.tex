\section{Related work} 

\textbf{Parameter settings related to weight decay.} 
\citet{Loshchilov2017DecoupledWD} explicitly highlights the coupling between the learning rate $\eta$ and the weight decay coefficient $\lambda$ in their study of Adam. For another parameter, \citet{blake2025umupunitscaledmaximalupdate} observe that, under fixed batch size and weight decay, the optimal learning rate decreases as the dataset size $N$ increases. This finding is broadly consistent with the results of \citet{wang2025setadamwsweightdecay}, who shows that larger datasets require a longer effective time scale $T$, which can be achieved by either reducing the weight decay or decreasing the learning rate. \citet{bergsma2025powerlinesscalinglaws} further demonstrates that the optimal weight decay coefficient scales linearly with the batch size $B$. Compared to directly tuning the learning rate, adjusting $\lambda$ provides improved stability in practice. In addition, \citet{fan2025robustlayerwisescalingrules} and \citet{wang2025setadamwsweightdecay} have shown that weight decay is critical to transfer efficacy. Similar to the conclusions given by \citet{wang2025setadamwsweightdecay}, \citet{dangelo2024needweightdecaymodern} observe that under under-training conditions, the optimal generalization performance of SGD often approximates $\eta \propto \frac{1}{\lambda}$. 

\textbf{Weight decay as a control on optimization dynamics.} A complementary line of work argues that in modern normalized networks the primary role of weight decay is not capacity control but the regulation of the effective learning rate. \citet{kosson2024rotational} show that for scale-invariant parameters the weight norm settles into a rotational equilibrium, where the shrinkage induced by weight decay balances the growth induced by the gradient; the equilibrium norm scales as $\sqrt{\eta/\lambda}$, and the magnitude of the relative update in the steady state depends only on the product $\eta\lambda$. \citet{kosson2025weightdecay} extend this to learning-rate transfer across model widths. \citet{xie2020stable} likewise modify how weight decay enters the update in order to stabilize its interaction with the learning rate.

We view this account as complementary to ours rather than competing, and it is worth stating precisely where the two differ. Rotational equilibrium is a \emph{steady-state} statement: it describes a stationary regime reached after a transient, and consequently the optimal product it prescribes carries no dependence on the training horizon $T$. \citet{kosson2025weightdecay} themselves observe that $(\eta,\lambda)$ pairs sharing a product still behave differently in the initial phase. Our stability argument instead bounds how far an iterate can be driven from a neighbouring trajectory over $T$ steps, and therefore predicts that the optimal product itself decays as $1/T$. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} tests this directly by varying the training length at fixed $\eta$, which is the one measurement that separates the two predictions.

The work most similar to ours is \cite{wang2025setadamwsweightdecay}. They investigate the parameter settings of the AdamW algorithm by one-to-one mapping from the EMA timescale to the weight decay hyperparameter, arriving at the same exponents in $\eta$ and $T$ that we obtain. Their argument is specific to the EMA reading of decoupled AdamW, whereas we reach the coupling for plain SGD and SGDM, where no EMA interpretation is available, and obtain in addition the constraint that weight decay tightens the admissible learning rate.
