\section{Experiments}\label{sec:experiments}


\textbf{Note}: The detailed experimental setups, methodology, cross-architecture/multi-seed robustness checks, and results are deferred to Appendix \ref{Appendix:exp} and \ref{Appendix:robustness}.

The following experiments empirically validate the theoretical results presented in Sections 4--5, translating them into actionable configuration strategies, including: optimal performance and learning rates of different optimizers (Exp.~1), $\eta$--$\lambda$ interaction (Exp.~2), $B$, $\lambda$, and $\eta$ coupling (Exp.~3), and their coupling in LLM fine-tuning (Exp.~4).

\textbf{Exp.\ 1: Optimal performance and corresponding LR.}  
Theory (Theorems \ref{thm:sgd-WD}, \ref{thm:sgdm-WD}) predicts better generalization and stricter learning rate bounds with weight decay (e.g., $\eta^*_{\text{SGD}} > \eta^*_{\text{SGD+WD}}$). Figure~\ref{fig:performance} confirms this: introducing $\lambda$ yields smaller generalization error and requires lower optimal learning rates. Table~\ref{tab:exp1} summarizes the best configurations: both SGD+WD and SGDM+WD achieve higher test accuracy than their plain counterparts, provided $\eta$ is properly scaled down.

\textbf{Exp.\ 2: $\eta$--$\lambda$ interaction.} 
We validate the inverse scaling law $\lambda=\mathcal{O}(1/\eta)$ (Eq. \ref{coupling:lamda}).
Figure~\ref{fig:WD and LR} shows the high-accuracy region forms a band consistent with $\eta \cdot \lambda \approx \text{const}$. Increasing $\eta$ while maintaining performance requires proportionally decreasing $\lambda$, and vice versa. Furthermore, plotting each fixed-$\lambda$ curve against the joint product $\eta\times\lambda$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:WD and LR}(b,c)) directly visualizes this law: all curves attain their lowest train loss and highest test accuracy inside the same narrow $\eta\times\lambda$ band.

\textbf{Exp.\ 3: $B$, $\lambda$, and $\eta$ coupling.}
We test the batch-size coupling (Eq.~\ref{coupling:Batch}) using the linear rule $\eta \propto B$ for $B \in \{32, 128, 256, 512\}$. Eq.~\eqref{coupling:Batch} predicts that under linear LR scaling, optimal $\lambda$ should increase with $B$. Figure~\ref{fig:batch} confirms that the optimal $\lambda$ and effective $\eta \cdot \lambda$ grow with batch size. When scaling batch size, both learning rate and weight decay must be scaled up to preserve generalization (e.g., the optimal $\lambda$ at $B{=}32$ shifts to a higher value at $B{=}128$).

% Testing the linear rule $\eta \propto B$ (Eq. \ref{coupling:Batch}), we find that scaling the batch size requires scaling both the learning rate and weight decay. The optimal $\lambda$ and the effective $\eta \cdot \lambda$ product inherently grow with $B$ to maintain peak performance.

\textbf{Exp.\ 4: Coupling among $B$, $\lambda$, and $\eta$ in LLM fine-tuning.}

We fine-tune Qwen3-0.6B with LoRA on MathInstruct and evaluate 0-shot out-of-distribution generalization on SVAMP, sweeping $\eta$ and $\lambda$ while holding LoRA configuration and optimization schedules fixed. 
% 
% As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:LLM1}, we validate the hyperparameter scaling law with batch size in the language modeling regime. For a fixed performance level, blue points (large batch size, $B=128$) consistently lie to the right of red points (small batch size, $B=32$). Since the horizontal axis represents increasing joint values of $\eta\times\lambda$, this demonstrates that larger batch sizes require proportionally larger joint values to achieve the same performance, confirming our stability scaling laws at a larger scale.
% 
We extend our findings to parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) using Qwen3-0.6B on MathInstruct, evaluated on SVAMP. Figure \ref{fig:LLM1} demonstrates that the $\eta$--$\lambda$ and batch size coupling hold in realistic LLM regimes. To achieve comparable performance when increasing batch size, the joint product $\eta \times \lambda$ must be proportionally increased.
