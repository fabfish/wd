\section{Conclusion}
In this work, we investigate the coupling between weight decay and other optimization hyperparameters from a generalization perspective. By establishing generalization bounds, we show that introducing the weight decay coefficient $\lambda$ disrupts the pre-existing hyperparameter equilibrium, necessitating a system-wide readjustment. Based on this, we propose the "Regularization Equivalence" hypothesis as a heuristic design principle. By aligning the stability profiles of explicit regularization (weight decay) and implicit regularization (SWA), we derive the explicit hyperparameter coupling $\lambda \approx C/S$ with $S=\sum_{t<T}\eta_t$, which reduces to $\lambda \approx C/(\eta T)$ for a constant step size and to $\lambda \approx 2C/(\eta T)$ under cosine annealing.
Furthermore, our analysis explicitly characterizes the role of batch size $B$ as a noise compensation mechanism: as $B$ increases, the reduction in implicit gradient noise must be counterbalanced by scaling up the joint configuration of $\lambda$ and $\eta$.
Empirically, we validated our heuristic theoretical findings and the proposed coupling across diverse architectures, ranging from ResNet-18, ResNet-50, and VGG-16 on vision tasks to Qwen3-0.6B on the language model.

\paragraph{Limitation.} 
Three limitations are worth stating plainly. First, the analysis assumes convexity and $L$-smoothness for tractability, and neither holds for the networks we train; what we claim transfers is not the theorem but two of its structural predictions, namely the shape of the learning-rate ceiling in $\lambda$ and the saturation of parameter divergence under weight decay, both of which we measure directly in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. Second, the coupling is a scaling relation with a fitted prefactor: the exponents follow from the analysis, but $C$ does not, and it varies by roughly a factor of two across the architectures and batch sizes we examine. Third, our language-model evidence uses LoRA fine-tuning, a setting in which weight decay is not a load-bearing hyperparameter and is frequently set to zero in practice; we therefore treat that result as suggestive rather than as validation, and regard full-parameter language-model training as the principal piece of missing evidence. Future work aims to rigorously extend this stability framework to non-convex settings.
